1 · Shape of the course

What the two days look like

The programme they sent is last October's Glenfield one, so treat the times as indicative. The structure will be the same.

Day 1 morning

Welcome and objectives. Indications and basics. A recap of the course reading. Coffee. Then the quiz with case discussion, which is Dr Kadri's 25 image slides.

Day 1 afternoon

Scope handling on models in two teams. Endoscope design and function. Cleaning and disinfection. Then hands-on in pairs with two trainers until about 16:30, and the groundwork for day 2 DOPS including ENTS.

Day 2

Hands-on training all morning. After lunch: the DOPS and accreditation process, unit management, then a skills review and discussion. Close about 16:45.

How to stand out. Faculty remember three things: the candidate who answered the drug cases correctly with the guideline name, the candidate who handled the scope calmly and talked through what they were doing, and the candidate who asked about DOPS and JETS before anyone else. Aim to be all three.

Course objectives, in their words. By the end you should be able to: list indications; describe diagnostic and therapeutic principles; obtain consent with a structured approach; explain patient preparation, throat spray versus sedation; identify who needs antibiotic prophylaxis; recognise patients at risk of complications and of bleeding, and plan anticoagulation around endoscopy; administer and monitor sedation safely; describe scope design and decontamination; explain accessories and diathermy; demonstrate intubation, manipulation and mucosal visualisation; and understand unit management.

2 · Dr Robinson's slide

Indications for OGD

Definite

  1. Age over 55 with recent onset dyspepsia (under one year) lasting more than 4 weeks.
  2. Dyspepsia with alarm symptoms: chronic GI bleeding, unintentional weight loss, progressive dysphagia, persistent vomiting, iron deficiency anaemia, epigastric mass. For anaemia the slide insists on both MCV under 80 fl and a low ferritin before you call it iron deficiency.
  3. Suspicious radiology: barium meal, CT, ultrasound.
  4. Haematemesis or melaena.

Possible

  • Confirming serology-positive coeliac disease with D2 biopsies.
  • Barrett's surveillance.

Not indicated

  • Under 55, no alarm features, and no adequate trial of acid suppression or no non-invasive H. pylori test yet.
  • Epigastric mass without dyspeptic symptoms. Image it first with ultrasound or CT.
Answer to have ready. "Under 55 without alarm features gets test-and-treat for H. pylori or a PPI trial first. Over 55 with new dyspepsia, or anyone with an alarm feature, gets a scope." That one sentence covers most of what they will ask.
3 · The consent talk

Consent and endoscopy

Their framing: consent is giving permission and keeping the right to withdraw it. It is a continuous process and much more than a signed form.

Valid consent needs three things

  • Capacity. Understands and retains the information, can weigh it and decide. Mental illness does not remove capacity. An irrational decision does not remove capacity.
  • Voluntariness. Without coercion. Their example: a patient who has taken bowel prep, is in a backless gown and is sitting on the edge of the trolley. Is that free to say no? Information given on arrival or in the room is weaker than information given in advance, so re-check acceptance of the risks.
  • Information. Nature of the procedure, the reason, the benefits, the risks, the alternatives including doing nothing, and the sedation or anaesthetic. Seriousness matters more than frequency: a serious complication affecting more than 1 in 1,000 must be mentioned.

Traps they listed

  • "I haven't got my glasses." "My hearing aid is outside." Fix it before you talk.
  • Translators: an independent one, not a friend, for anything that matters.
  • Consent under sedation is not consent. Take it before the first drug.
  • Adults without capacity and no proxy: best-interests decision. The Mental Health Act does not authorise treatment of physical conditions. A sectioned patient still needs consent or a best-interests process.
  • Never ask relatives to sign for an adult.

Their three scenarios, with the answers to give

A 45-year-old accountant with epigastric pain and night-time heartburn suggestive of reflux.
Capacity and voluntariness are not in doubt. The question is whether the procedure is indicated at all: under 55, no alarm features, so ask about the PPI trial and H. pylori test first. If he comes to endoscopy, give written information in advance, go through risks including perforation, bleeding, the sedation risks and the throat spray alternative, and record his choice. He can withdraw at any point, including on the trolley.
A confused 75-year-old woman after a stroke who keeps pulling out NG tubes, referred for a PEG.
Capacity assessment first, for this decision, today. If she lacks capacity: no proxy can consent for her, so it is a best-interests decision involving the team, the family's view of what she would have wanted, and any advance decision or lasting power of attorney. PEG in post-stroke dysphagia is a value judgement about prognosis, not a reflex. Document the process.
A 43-year-old man with alcoholic liver disease, fresh haematemesis, struggling during an attempted OGD.
An emergency, so consent is proportionate but still real: explain what you are doing and why in plain words, check he agrees, and stop if he withdraws. If he cannot cooperate safely, the answer is not more force; it is to stop, resuscitate, give terlipressin and antibiotics, and consider airway protection with anaesthetic support before a second attempt.
4 · The main reading: BSG and ESGE 2021 guideline

Antiplatelets and anticoagulants

This is the session where you can be visibly better prepared than the room. The whole guideline reduces to one grid: how risky is the procedure, and how risky is stopping the drug.

First, always: tell the patient the thrombotic risk of stopping and the bleeding risk of continuing. That advice is itself a strong recommendation in the guideline.

Their three cases, answered

Case 1. 68-year-old man with IBD on aspirin after a TIA. Referred for flexible sigmoidoscopy and a biopsy series.
Go ahead and take the biopsies. Aspirin is continued for all endoscopic procedures. The slide's own evidence: a case-control study of more than 20,000 colonoscopies with polypectomy showed no increase in haemorrhage on aspirin. Take as many biopsies as the series needs.
Case 2. 71-year-old woman, bloating and diarrhoea, weakly positive coeliac serology, previous CVA with full recovery, on clopidogrel and an ACE inhibitor. Referred for D2 biopsies.
Do not stop the clopidogrel. Biopsy anyway. Diagnostic OGD with biopsies is a low-risk procedure and P2Y12 inhibitors are continued. The older slide's "stop 7 days in low-risk conditions" refers to therapeutic procedures; for diagnostic work with multiple biopsies it says safe. Take four biopsies from D2 and one or two from the bulb, on a gluten-containing diet.
Case 3. 65-year-old man with weight loss, nausea and epigastric pain. AF on warfarin, INR 2.4.
Continue the warfarin, go ahead, and biopsy any ulcer. Diagnostic OGD with biopsy is low risk, and 2.4 is within range. Do not start heparin. If a finding needed a high-risk therapy (for example dilating a stricture), that would be a planned second procedure: stop warfarin 5 days before, INR under 1.5, and for plain AF without the high-risk features, no bridging.

Acute upper GI bleeding on these drugs

  • Haemodynamically unstable on warfarin: reverse with prothrombin complex concentrate plus vitamin K; do not wait for the INR to fall before scoping.
  • Dabigatran can be reversed with idarucizumab; the factor Xa drugs with andexanet where available. Most DOAC bleeds settle with time and endoscopic therapy.
  • Aspirin for secondary prevention is continued or restarted within days. Anticoagulation is usually restarted within 7 days once haemostasis is secure, because the thrombotic risk of staying off it is real.
The classic trap. The quiz loves the patient on dual antiplatelets with a recent drug-eluting stent. Stopping both before an elective procedure risks stent thrombosis. The answer is defer if elective; if not, keep the aspirin and talk to cardiology about the P2Y12 drug.
5 · BSG 2009 antibiotic guideline

Antibiotic prophylaxis

The headline is a negative: prophylaxis against infective endocarditis is no longer recommended for any diagnostic or therapeutic endoscopy, prosthetic valves included. Bacteraemia is common after dilatation and sclerotherapy, endocarditis is extremely rare, and there is no evidence prophylaxis prevents it. If a cardiac-risk patient develops a fever after endoscopy, think of endocarditis and take cultures.

Where antibiotics are still given

  • PEG insertion. A single IV dose before the procedure (co-amoxiclav or cefuroxime; teicoplanin if MRSA-colonised or penicillin-allergic) reduces peristomal infection.
  • Cirrhosis with any GI bleed. Antibiotics for every patient, because they reduce infection, rebleeding and death.
  • ERCP where drainage may be incomplete (hilar strictures, sclerosing cholangitis, pseudocysts) and in biliary complications after transplant.
  • EUS-guided FNA of pancreatic cystic lesions.
  • Severe neutropenia or profound immunosuppression undergoing a high-risk procedure: consider it.

Not routinely for dilatation, variceal sclerotherapy or banding in a non-bleeding patient, or diagnostic procedures of any kind.

6 · Patient preparation

Throat spray, sedation and monitoring

Fasting

Solids 6 hours. Clear fluids 2 hours. Diabetics early on the list. Gastroparesis or achalasia: longer, and consider the airway risk.

Throat spray alone

Lidocaine 10% spray to the oropharynx. Patient stays awake, fully in control, drives home. Trade-off: more gagging, more retching during intubation. Nil by mouth until the throat sensation returns, about an hour.

Conscious sedation

Midazolam titrated in 1 to 2 mg steps to a drowsy but rousable patient who obeys commands. Keep the total modest, and much lower in the elderly and frail. If combining with an opioid, give the fentanyl first and reduce the midazolam. Cannot drive for 24 hours and needs an escort.

Monitoring

Pulse oximetry throughout, supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula for sedated patients, a dedicated nurse watching the patient rather than the screen. Record the doses and the observations. Recovery until awake with stable observations.

Reversal

Flumazenil for midazolam, naloxone for opioids. Every use of a reversal agent is recorded and audited; the unit's GRS measures it. Needing reversal is a sedation-dose error, not a routine step.

Who is high risk

Elderly, frail, cardiorespiratory disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, chronic opioid or benzodiazepine use, hepatic impairment, and anyone with a likely difficult airway. Lower doses, slower titration, anaesthetic help for the sickest.

If they ask "spray or sedation?" Offer the choice with honest pros and cons; many straightforward diagnostic OGDs are done with spray alone, and the patient's preference and comorbidity decide.
7 · The model and the hands-on sessions

Scope handling and a complete examination

Holding the scope

  • Left hand holds the control body. Thumb on the large wheel: up and down. Thumb also reaches the small wheel: left and right. Index and middle fingers on the two valves: the upper valve is suction, the lower is air and water (cover it for air, press it for water). Lock the small wheel when you need both hands.
  • Right hand holds the shaft about 20 to 30 cm from the mouth, close to the bite guard, and does the pushing, pulling and torque. Most steering is torque plus the big wheel, not the small wheel.
  • Keep the shaft straight between you and the patient. Loops in the shaft steal your torque.

Intubation

  1. Patient in left lateral, chin slightly down towards the chest, bite guard in, a nurse holding it.
  2. Scope tip over the tongue in the midline, following the curve of the tongue down. Keep the lumen in view. The epiglottis and the vocal cords appear; the cords are a landmark, not a target.
  3. Pass behind the arytenoids, usually to the left of the midline, to the cricopharyngeus. Ask the patient to swallow and advance with gentle pressure as it relaxes. If it does not open, wait and ask for another swallow. Never push blind and never push hard.
  4. Into the oesophagus with a little air. Say what you see as you go.

The J manoeuvre (retroflexion)

In the body of the stomach, with a good lumen, turn the big wheel fully up, add a little left, and withdraw gently. The scope shaft appears coming through the cardia. Rotate to see the whole cardia and fundus. Straighten the tip before you withdraw out of the stomach. Cardia cancers, fundal varices and hiatus hernias are all retroflexion findings.

The photo set assessors expect

A complete OGD is documented with a standard set of images, and the DOPS marks visualisation and documentation. Take them in this order on the way out, each clean and in focus:

  1. Upper oesophagus
  2. 2 cm above the GOJ
  3. Z-line
  4. Cardia and fundus in retroflexion
  5. Gastric body
  6. Incisura in retroflexion
  7. Antrum
  8. Duodenal bulb
  9. D2
On the model and the patient, narrate. "Cricopharyngeus, waiting for a swallow. In the oesophagus, Z-line at 39. Pool suctioned, following the greater curve. Pylorus, waiting for it to open. Bulb, now the corkscrew into D2. Withdrawing: D2, bulb, antrum, incisura on retroflexion, cardia, body, Z-line, oesophagus, done." Trainers hear control, and so do you.

Biopsies and basic therapeutics

  • Forceps through the working channel, open, press the mucosa, close, pull. Edge of an ulcer, not the base. Six to eight from a suspicious lesion, four from D2 plus one or two from the bulb for coeliac, a Sydney-protocol set for H. pylori if your unit uses it.
  • Diathermy basics: cutting current is a continuous waveform and cuts cleanly with little coagulation; coagulation current is pulsed and seals vessels with more thermal spread; blend sits between. Safety: the return pad on a well-perfused muscle, no metal contact, know the pacemaker and ICD rules, keep the tip away from the lens, keep the lumen insufflated so you are not burning the wall you cannot see.
8 · Dr Kadri's session

Endoscope design and decontamination

  • Parts: light guide connector, umbilical cord, control body with the two wheels and two valves, insertion tube with the bending section and the distal tip. The tip carries the objective lens, two light guides, the air and water nozzle, and the working channel opening.
  • Channels: air and water share a channel to the nozzle; suction and the instrument share the working channel. That is why the suction valve clears the view and why a forceps in the channel blocks suction.
  • Image: a charge-coupled device at the tip sends the picture to the processor; the light comes from a xenon or LED source through fibre bundles.
  • Decontamination: bedside pre-clean immediately after the case (wipe the insertion tube, flush the channels, suck detergent through), then leak test, then manual cleaning with brushes through every channel, then automated endoscope reprocessing with high-level disinfection, rinse, dry, and storage in a drying cabinet with a traceability record linking scope, patient and washer cycle. Single-use accessories are single use. Reusable accessories go through sterilisation, not just disinfection.
  • Why it matters: cross-infection outbreaks have been traced to inadequate channel brushing and wet storage. The unit audits reprocessing as part of GRS.
9 · Day 2 afternoon

Unit management and the GRS

The Global Rating Scale from JAG measures how well a unit delivers patient-centred care in four domains: patient experience, clinical quality, workforce, training. Each item is graded by maturity:

LevelMeaning
DNationally agreed standards are published in the department
CThere are processes to monitor the standards
BAction is agreed when performance falls below the standards
AThe action taken is reviewed to make sure it worked

Lines of management are a medical or surgical lead, a nursing lead, and a users' group that brings leads, stakeholders, managers and accountants together. BSG quality and safety indicators the slide listed: a reporting system with image capture, agreed antibiotic, anticoagulation and sedation policies, supervised trainees, operator-specific audit, appropriate monitoring and resuscitation equipment, adverse-event monitoring, audit of reversal agents and 30-day mortality.

Operational topics: list composition (urgents, inpatients, soons, therapeutics, training), cancellation rules, vetting of referrals, full versus partial booking, servicing and stock, same-sex recovery, information and reports, staffing numbers and rotas, and governance through the users' group: waiting-list targets including 2-week-wait, complications audit, patient satisfaction, protocols and training needs.

Something only you can say in that session. You built the live rota and on-call tool your department uses, and you ran the 2-week-wait triage audit. When unit management comes up, one sentence about either makes you memorable.

11 · Day 2 afternoon and JETS

DOPS, ENTS and what assessors look for

JAG certification runs on formative DOPS entered on JETS after supervised procedures, then summative DOPS when you are near competence. The course gives you your first ones. Knowing the form before they explain it is the single easiest way to look ahead of the group.

Pre-procedure

Indication checked, consent taken properly, safety checklist, drugs and allergies, anticoagulants reviewed, equipment checked.

Insertion

Safe intubation, keeping a luminal view, scope manipulation with torque and wheels, managing patient comfort and the airway.

During the procedure

Complete mucosal visualisation including retroflexion and D2, lesion recognition, appropriate biopsies, photo documentation.

After

An accurate report, a plan, instructions to the patient and to the referrer, histology follow-up.

ENTS

Endoscopic non-technical skills: situation awareness, decision making, communication and teamwork, leadership. Talking to the patient and the nurse through the case scores here.

Scoring

Each item is rated by how much supervision was needed. The aim across training is consistent "no supervision required" ratings before summative sign-off.

Questions worth asking on the day

  • "Will the course DOPS be entered on JETS, and who is my course trainer on the form?"
  • "What are the current JAG requirements for OGD certification: numbers, DOPS, the KPI thresholds for D2 intubation and retroflexion?"
  • "How do you run your training lists, and what would you want a trainee to have done before the first supervised patient?"
Behaviour that gets you remembered. Introduce yourself to the nurse and the model patient every time. Say your plan before you pick up the scope. Ask for feedback after each go and write it down in front of them. Thank the trainers by name.
